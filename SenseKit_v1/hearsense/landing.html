The problem

Hearing loss is huge — and caught far too late.

People wait years before testing. Clinical pure-tone audiometry needs a soundproof booth, calibrated equipment and an audiologist — out of reach for most, especially in rural areas.

1.5 billion

people live with some hearing loss worldwide (WHO).

How it works

Listen. Repeat. Track.

HearSense checks your room is quiet enough, then plays spoken digits in noise and adapts the difficulty to find your threshold.

1

Quiet check

The mic measures background noise and blocks the test if it’s too loud — the quality gate most apps skip.

2

Listen

You hear digit triplets (e.g. 4-2-7) mixed with noise, one ear at a time.

3

Adapt

Get it right and the noise gets louder; wrong and it eases — homing in on your signal-to-noise threshold.

4

Track

Store the result, plot the trend, and get a plain-language explanation + referral advice.

Example output
“Speech-in-noise score: −6 dB SNR — within normal range.”
“Right ear: likely mild loss in high frequencies (2–4 kHz).”
“High-frequency hearing dropped vs last month — see an audiologist.”
“Room too noisy (58 dB) — move somewhere quieter.”

Recommended · no calibration needed

🔢 Digits-in-Noise

Repeat spoken digits in noise. Highly correlated with clinical audiometry, works on any device/headphones.

  • Adaptive signal-to-noise threshold (dB SNR)
  • Per-ear testing
  • WHO-style approach (hearWHO)
  • ~3 minutes · robust to hardware differences
Frequency profile

🎚️ Pure-tone sweep

Find the softest tone you can hear across frequencies to sketch an audiogram-style profile.

  • 500 Hz – 8 kHz across both ears
  • Visual audiogram output
  • Best with headphones in a quiet room
  • Shown as a relative screen (not clinical dB HL)
